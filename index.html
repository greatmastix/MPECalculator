How this is calculated

  • What the results rest on: the limits follow the named standards; everything the data sheet does not pin down (beam profile, line-end dwell, light spectrum, lens hot spots, zoom fall-off) uses typical real-world values from published measurements. Measured values of your own unit (power through a 7 mm aperture, beam size, pulse width, lux at a distance) give the most reliable answer, and the beam-profile and lens settings let you choose the worst case instead.
  • Limits: eye MPE for 400 to 700 nm and a point source, IEC 60825-1:2014 (identical to ICNIRP 2013): 2 mJ/m² up to 5 µs, 18·t0.75 J/m² up to 10 s (25.5 W/m² = 2.55 mW/cm² at 0.25 s), then 10 W/m². From 10 s the photochemical limit (100·CB J/m², then CB W/m²) is checked for 400 to 600 nm and the lower one counts. Thermal exposure adds over the colours; photochemical adds each colour against its own limit. The 2007 edition and EU Directive 2006/25/EC have the same CW limits and older pulse rules (5 mJ/m² up to 18 µs, C5 = N−0.25 over every pulse).
  • Beam: irradiance is averaged over a 7 mm pupil. Diameter and divergence are converted to d63 from their own definitions; d63² = (a·(1 + D·r))² + (φ·r)² with a lens of power D at the aperture. Show lasers are multimode diodes, flat along one axis and near-Gaussian along the other; the typical profile sits halfway (geometric mean) between a Gaussian and a round flat-top beam of the same size, which delivers 1.1 to 1.4 times the Gaussian value into the pupil. Choose flat-top for the worst case, Gaussian for single-mode lasers. The beam is taken as narrowest at the aperture; for a projector focused further out, measure distances from the narrowest point.
  • Scanning: every spot of the pattern is checked and the worst counts. A line crossing is a pulse whose energy and FWHM come from the exact integral of the beam passing the 7 mm aperture at the beam speed (path angle × frame rate × distance). At a line end the beam rests for the anchor points plus about two points of galvo lag, and the passes in and out merge into one pulse. Beam dots last points ÷ scan rate. Each spot must meet the single-pulse limit (with C5 for N pulses within 10 s) and the average over the exposure time, which also covers every shorter group of pulses. Pulses shorter than 5 µs are also assessed as 5 µs pulses of the same peak power (IEC 60825-1:2014 ISH1) and the lower result counts. Real galvos can lag more at sharp turns; a measured pulse width is the reliable check. The scanned result relies on a working scan-fail safeguard; the scan-fail tile shows what a stopped beam delivers in its reaction time (ILDA: 10 ms, preferably 1 ms; DGUV/DIN 56912: at most 100 ms).
  • Exposure time: 0.25 s is the blink and aversion response IEC 60825-1 and ICNIRP use for accidental exposure. Germany's DGUV Information 203-036 does not accept it for laser shows: it asks for at least 4 s per event (deliberate eye closing and turning away) and compliance over the whole show (for example 15 min).
  • Lens and reduction: a diverging lens of power D adds a divergence of a·D. The lens shown is the total lens on the laser's own beam; lenses in contact add. Percentages are of full power.
  • Dazzle distance (NODD): the distance out to which the beam, resting on the eye, hides a patch of the view of the given width. Simplified maximum dazzle exposure table of Williamson and McLin (2018) at 555 nm, in µW/cm²: 2° 0.001 / 0.6 / 40, 10° 0.04 / 30 / 2,000, 20° 0.16 / 120 / 8,000, 40° 0.6 / 450 / 30,000 for night / dusk / day, each colour weighted by the CIE 2008 eye sensitivity the framework specifies. The table is for a 40-year-old with dark eyes looking for a small, faint object (0.08° at 60 % contrast); older or light-eyed viewers are dazzled further out, roughly 1.2 to 1.5 times the distance at 65. Loss in the air is ignored.
  • Skin: the skin limit is at least 50 times higher than the eye MPE, so it is met wherever the eye MPE is met. It only matters for people whose eyes are protected.
  • Not covered: wavelengths outside 400 to 700 nm, pulsed (Q-switched) lasers, viewing through binoculars or camera lenses, reflections, atmospheric scintillation on long outdoor paths.
  • Risk group: risk group 2 is acceptable for an audience, risk group 3 is not. The verdict and the hazard distance use the RG2/RG3 boundary, a 0.25 s look, under the selected standard. The exposure time you enter is for how long someone can stare into the fixture.
  • IEC 62471-5:2015 / ICNIRP 2013: retinal thermal radiance (R(λ) weighting 380 to 1400 nm) ≤ 28,000 / α W/m²/sr from 0.25 s, 20,000 / (α·t0.25) below; continuous light is averaged over 11 mrad and α is taken at least 11 mrad and at most 100 mrad. Blue light: weighted radiance × time ≤ 10⁶ J/m²/sr, averaged over 11 mrad up to 100 s, then 1.1·√t mrad.
  • IEC 62471:2006 / EN 62471 / EU 2006/25/EC: the older ICNIRP 1997 weighting (10 × B(λ) in the blue) and 50,000 / (C·t0.25) W/m²/sr up to 10 s, C = α between 1.7 and 100 mrad, with a 1.7 mrad field of view at 0.25 s. The Directive itself gives no averaging angle for retinal thermal; this uses the EN 62471 / CIE S009 reading, while the Commission's non-binding guide simplifies to shorter distances. For small, blue-rich beam sources this gives much longer hazard distances than IEC 62471-5; many data sheets and EU workplace assessments use it.
  • Source model: the front lens is an evenly lit disc of luminance = peak intensity ÷ lens area, raised by the hot-spot factor where the lens is larger than the averaging angle (measured: about 1 for profiles at narrow zoom and even optics, up to about 2 for washes and wide zoom; typical 1.5). Close to the fixture the eye sees only a patch of the lens, limited to 1.2 × the beam angle. A negative beam angle makes the crossover the source. Wider zoom lowers the peak intensity along a typical curve fitted to the published zoom photometry of 36 fixtures (with lux at both ends it follows the data). Lux from a photometric table is converted as lux × distance² from the front lens. Below 4° beam angle, lumens do not give the peak intensity reliably (0.3 to 5 times in published data), so lux or candela is needed.
  • Projectors: peak intensity = ANSI lumens ÷ the solid angle of the image (from throw ratio and aspect ratio); the source is the lens aperture (focal length ÷ F-number), evenly lit, and follows the zoom. Checked against Panasonic's published IEC 62471-5 hazard distances (PT-RZ21K, PT-RZ12K, PT-RQ25K, PT-RZ970 with their lenses): within −16 % to +13 %. Hazard distances are measured from the lens.
  • Spectrum by light engine (lumens per R-weighted watt for ICNIRP 2013 / 1997, blue light per lumen), typical values: white LED 314 (285 at 10,000 K) / 190 to 72 by colour temperature, blue light 0.9 × black body; violet-pumped or unknown LED 245, blue light × 1.45; laser-phosphor 300 (model-based), blue light 0.9 × black body; discharge lamp in a moving head 270 / 78; open-face HMI as a black body including its infrared, at most 200, blue light × 1.4; xenon short arc 130 (an unverified estimate); tungsten as a full black body including its infrared; RGB mixes 140 / 24 with 3.7 mW of blue light per lumen (Claypaky Xtylos measured at full RGB: 140 lm/W, 3.1 mW/lm). Sources: CIE 015:2018 LED spectra, DGUV BoSS spectroradiometer tables of 13 moving heads.
  • PWM dimming: every pulse runs at full brightness, so with a PWM frequency each pulse is also checked against the ICNIRP 2013 repetitive-pulse rule (pulse limit × n−0.25, at least 0.4 or 0.2) for sources larger than 5 mrad. Without a frequency the dimmer is taken as current dimming.
  • Dazzle distance (NODD): the illuminance at the eye in lux is compared with the same maximum dazzle exposure table as on the laser page as lux ÷ 683, raised by about 5 % because the framework's CIE 2008 eye sensitivity rates white light slightly brighter than lux does. Open white is assumed. Loss in the air is ignored.
  • Not covered: UV and infrared limits, heat on skin and materials close to the lens (see the fixture's minimum distance to lit objects).
